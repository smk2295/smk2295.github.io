Paper notes — In-Context Learning as Implicit Bayesian Inference

Created in August 09, 2025

2025   ·   uncertainty   in-context-learning   theory   ·   paper-notes

Xie, S. M., Raghunathan, A., Liang, P., & Ma, T. (2022). An Explanation of In-Context Learning as Implicit Bayesian Inference. ICLR 2022.

The Puzzle

In-context learning has no obvious mechanism: no gradient step separates seeing the demonstrations from producing the answer. The paper asks under what pretraining conditions this behavior should be expected to emerge from a plain next-token-prediction objective, rather than treated as an unexplained empirical curiosity.

A Synthetic Pretraining Distribution

The authors construct a pretraining distribution generated by a mixture of Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), where each HMM instance corresponds to a distinct latent document concept:

  • A document is generated by first sampling a concept, then sampling a long token sequence from that concept’s HMM.
  • Within one document, the concept stays fixed — meant to mimic how real documents keep a consistent topic or style throughout.

They prove that a Transformer trained to predict the next token under this generative process is, in the large-pretraining-data limit, implicitly performing Bayesian inference over which latent concept generated the current document, conditioned only on the tokens observed so far.

Why This Predicts In-Context Learning

At test time, an ICL prompt — a concatenation of demonstrations — looks to the pretrained model like a partial document:

  • Each demonstration is additional evidence about which latent concept is active.
  • Conditioning on more demonstrations narrows the posterior over concepts.
  • The model’s next-token prediction under that sharpened posterior is what looks, from the outside, like “learning the task from examples.”

No parameter update is needed for any of this — the update is Bayesian, and it happens entirely within the forward pass, over the model’s belief about which concept applies.

Empirical Support: GINC

The theory is paired with experiments on a synthetic benchmark the authors call GINC (Generative IN-Context learning dataset), built directly from an HMM mixture matching the theory’s assumptions.

  • Accuracy on the synthetic ICL task increases with the number of demonstrations, and is fairly robust to demonstration ordering — both predicted directly by the Bayesian account.
  • Real pretrained LMs (GPT-2, GPT-3) show qualitatively similar demonstration-count scaling on natural ICL tasks, though the authors present this as suggestive rather than as proof that real LMs satisfy the paper’s formal assumptions exactly.

Takeaways

  1. ICL doesn’t require a new learning mechanism — under the right pretraining distribution, it’s a byproduct of ordinary next-token prediction.
  2. The object the model infers from demonstrations is a latent concept, not a new function learned on the fly.
  3. This gives uncertainty a precise meaning in the ICL setting: the posterior over concepts is not concentrated on one option. That distinguishes an ICL failure caused by genuinely ambiguous demonstrations (a property of the evidence — aleatoric) from one caused by a poorly calibrated posterior even given clear evidence (a property of the model — epistemic).
  4. This is the theoretical anchor for treating ICL uncertainty as uncertainty over a latent variable at all, rather than only as variance in the model’s final output.
